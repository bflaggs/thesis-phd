\chapter{Cosmic Ray Astrophysics}\label{chap:cosmicrays}
Cosmic rays are ultra-relativistic charged particles of astrophysical origin.
They were discovered in 1912 when Victor Hess, armed with his electroscope and a daring idea, performed several high-altitude hot-air balloon flights to study radiation in the atmosphere~\cite{victorhess_1912}.
Hess measured increasing levels of atmospheric radiation as his balloon rose in altitude up to \qty{5}{\kilo\meter}, leading him to hypothesize the existence of a ``radiation of cosmic origin'', to which he gave the name cosmic rays.
He was later awarded the physics Nobel prize in 1936 for his discovery and the misnomer given to these particles of astrophysical origin stuck.

Cosmic rays have been studied for over a century since their discovery, yet the acceleration mechanisms producing ultra-high-energy cosmic rays, those above \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{18}}{\electronvolt} in energy, and their astrophysical sources are still a mystery.
There are several measurable properties of cosmic rays that help to provide progress towards answering this mystery, such as their energies, mass composition, and arrival directions at Earth.
Furthermore, cosmic rays are the highest energy subatomic particles in the known Universe, with center of mass energies exceeding the most energetic collisions at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) by nearly three orders of magnitude.
Hence, ultra-high-energy cosmic rays push the frontiers of both astrophysics and particle physics simultaneously.
This chapter will mostly discuss the astrophysical implications of cosmic-ray physics and~\Cref{chap:airshowers} will present the particle physics components; however, they are inherently coupled, with aspects of each physics sub-field in both chapters.

\section{Energy Spectrum}\label{sec:spectrum}
Cosmic rays span over 10 orders of magnitude in energy, starting from \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{9}}{\electronvolt} up to nearly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{21}}{\electronvolt} and possibly beyond, with the highest-energy cosmic ray measured at an energy of \qty[parse-numbers=false]{3.2$\times$10^{20}}{\electronvolt} by the Fly's Eye detector in 1991~\cite{flyseye_prl1995_omg}.
The flux of these cosmic particles arriving at Earth above an energy $E$, per unit area $A$, per solid angle $\Omega$, and per time $t$, is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Phi(E) = \frac{\text{d}N}{\text{d}A\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t}. \label{eq:flux}
\end{equation}

\noindent
The energy-dependent flux is typically reported as a differential flux $J$ that follows a power law distribution:

\begin{equation}
    J(E) = \frac{\text{d}\Phi(E)}{\text{d}E} = \frac{\text{d}N}{\text{d}A\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}E} \propto E^{-\gamma}, \label{eq:differential_flux}
\end{equation}

\noindent
with $\gamma$ an energy-dependent spectral index, constrained to $\gamma>0$.
Shown in~\Cref{fig:spectrum_measurements} is the differential flux of cosmic rays, i.e. the cosmic-ray energy spectrum, above \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{15}}{\electronvolt} in energy as measured by various cosmic-ray experiments.
At these ultra-high energies, cosmic rays are extremely rare, with a flux of \qty{1}{\per\meter\squared\per\year} at roughly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{16}}{\electronvolt} and \qty{1}{\per\kilo\meter\squared\per\year} at nearly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{19}}{\electronvolt}.
Measuring ultra-high-energy cosmic rays with meaningful statistics therefore requires large observatories covering detection areas of several hundreds to thousands of square kilometers.
The impact on cosmic-ray measurements are large measurement uncertainties and only few experiments (The Pierre Auger Observatory and the Telescope Array experiment) with measurements at the highest energies of the cosmic-ray energy spectrum.
In the figure, the differential flux is scaled by $E^{3}$ to highlight features in the cosmic-ray energy spectrum.

The spectral features are deviations from a constant power law and are described by changes in the energy-dependent spectral index $\gamma$.
The primary features of the energy spectrum are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Knee}: a spectral softening from $\gamma$\,=\,2.74 to $\gamma$\,=\,3.13 at an energy of \\\qty[parse-numbers=false]{(3.67\,$\pm$\,0.05\,$\pm$\,0.15)$\times$10^{15}}{\electronvolt} as measured by LHAASO~\cite{lhaaso_prl2024_spectrum}.
    \item \textit{Second Knee}: a spectral softening from $\gamma$\,=\,2.85 to $\gamma$\,=\,3.28 at an energy of \\\qty[parse-numbers=false]{(1.58\,$\pm$\,0.05\,$\pm$\,0.2)$\times$10^{17}}{\electronvolt} measured from the combined spectrum fit of the \qty{750}{\meter} and \qty{1500}{\meter} spaced particle detector array measurements with the fluorescence telescope measurements of the Pierre Auger Observatory~\cite{icrc2021_novotny_spectrum}.
    \item \textit{Ankle}: a spectral hardening from $\gamma$\,=\,3.25 to $\gamma$\,=\,2.51 at an energy of \\\qty[parse-numbers=false]{(5.1\,$\pm$\,0.1\,$\pm$\,1.1)$\times$10^{18}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{auger_prl2025_spectrum}.
    \item \textit{Instep}: a flattening of the $E^{3}$ weighted spectrum, resulting from a spectral index change of $\gamma$\,=\,2.51 to $\gamma$\,=\,2.99 at an energy of \qty[parse-numbers=false]{(1.3\,$\pm$\,0.1\,$\pm$\,0.2)$\times$10^{19}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{auger_prl2025_spectrum}.
    \item \textit{Cutoff}: an extreme suppression in the cosmic-ray flux, changing the spectral index from $\gamma$\,=\,2.99 to $\gamma$\,=\,5.4 at an energy of \qty[parse-numbers=false]{(4.8\,$\pm$\,0.2\,$\pm$\,0.5)$\times$10^{19}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{auger_prl2025_spectrum}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The knee of the cosmic-ray energy spectrum has been observed by several other cosmic-ray observatories to occur at approximately \qty[parse-numbers=false]{3$\times$10^{15}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{tibet3_apj2008_spectrum, tunka_nima2014_spectrum, icecube_prd2020_spectrum}, in agreement with the highly precise LHAASO results.
The second knee has also been measured using only the \qty{433}{\meter} spaced particle detector array of the Pierre Auger Observatory, yielding a cosmic-ray energy of \qty[parse-numbers=false]{(2.3\,$\pm$\,0.5)$\times$10^{17}}{\electronvolt} for the spectral feature~\cite{icrc2023_gaby_spectrum}; however, this feature is not a sharp break but rather an extended softening which occurs between a range of \qtyrange[parse-numbers=false]{1$\times$10^{17}}{2$\times$10^{17}}{\electronvolt}.
The given energies of the ankle, instep, and cutoff spectral features were determined using Phase I measurements of the Pierre Auger Observatory and shown to be mostly declination independent~\cite{auger_prl2025_spectrum}.
Phase II Auger measurements are in agreement with the Phase I results, within uncertainties~\cite{icrc2025_diego_spectrum}.
The Telescope Array experiment reports energies of the ankle, instep, and cutoff spectral features which generally agree with the Pierre Auger Observatory results within systematic uncertainties and accounting for the energy scale difference between the experiments~\cite{icrc2025_ta_spectrum}.
There is tension between the two experiments regarding the spectral cutoff feature, in both the energy of the cutoff and the post-cutoff spectral index.
Yet, this is hypothesized to be caused from differences in the analysis method rather than astrophysics~\cite{icrc2025_augerta_spectrum}.

Overall, measurements of these spectral features are in agreement between cosmic-ray experiments, yet the exact astrophysical cause for each feature is still unknown.

\section{Mass Composition}\label{sec:mass}
Astrophysical acceleration and propagation processes are energy dependent, leading to an energy dependence on the mass composition of cosmic rays.
Therefore, inherent to interpreting the astrophysical meaning of the spectral features is determining the mass of the incoming cosmic rays, both the average mass composition and, hopefully with future work, on a per-event basis.
The latter would allow for charged particle astronomy, which when used with particle backtracking would point towards cosmic-ray sources.
Direct mass measurements of incoming cosmic rays have been made using both balloon and satellite instruments for cosmic rays below approximately \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{15}}{\electronvolt}.
At higher energies, the cosmic-ray flux becomes too small for feasible measurements with small scale detectors, and instead requires large-scale ground based detector systems.
For ground-based observatories, direct cosmic-ray mass measurements are no longer possible as the primary cosmic ray will interact with atmospheric molecules to initiate an extensive air shower of secondary particles and radiation that propagate through the atmosphere until reaching the ground.
Indirect cosmic-ray observations occur for cosmic-ray energies above nearly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{14}}{\electronvolt}, complicating mass measurements above this energy threshold.

Ground-based observatories instead determine the mass composition of cosmic rays by reconstructing air-shower properties which are sensitive to the primary cosmic-ray mass.
One heavily studied property is the atmospheric depth of shower maximum, referred to as $X_{\rm max}$, and known to be correlated with primary particle mass.
The average $X_{\rm max}$ and its fluctuations, as measured by various ground-based experiments, are presented in~\Cref{fig:mean_and_sigma_xmax}.
Data used for these figures are available from the references within~\cite{snowmass_2023_uhecr}.
Clearly visible are the different predicted values of the $X_{\rm max}$ distributions from various hadronic interaction models.
Therefore, interpretation of air-shower data is heavily dependent on the assumed hadronic interaction model.
This generates an additional systematic uncertainty in the data interpretation as these models are extrapolated into an energy regime beyond LHC energies where the handling of hadronic interactions with full precision is not yet known.
A description of air showers and their properties with more depth is given in~\Cref{chap:airshowers}.

\section{Anisotropy}\label{sec:anisotropy}
The large scale structure of the Universe is assumed to be isotropic on length scales greater than roughly \qty{100}{\mega\parsec}, with the potential cosmic-ray source distribution following the assumed homogeneity beyond this ``horizon''.
Yet, at distances greater than \qty{100}{\mega\parsec} from Earth, cosmic rays above roughly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{18}}{\electronvolt} are subject to energy loss effects from propagation (see~\Cref{sec:propagation}).
This requires the highest energy cosmic rays reaching Earth to be generated in a region where the homogeneity assumption of the Universe breaks down, resulting in an expected deviation from isotropy in ultra-high-energy cosmic ray arrival directions at Earth.
Studying cosmic-ray arrival directions for anisotropies can then provide evidence of sources for the most energetic charged particles in the known Universe.
Combined with event-by-event mass measurements and extensive Galactic and extragalactic magnetic field models, charged particle astronomy is possible which would determine individual sources of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays.

In 2017, the Pierre Auger Observatory measured a dipolar anisotropy in the arrival direction of cosmic rays above \qty{8}{\exa\electronvolt} with a 5.2$\sigma$ significance in the direction pointing away from the Galactic center, supporting theories that the highest energy cosmic rays are of extragalactic origin~\cite{auger_science2017_anisotropy}.
Recently, the observatory updated their analysis with increased statistics and reported an increase in this dipolar anisotropy to 6.8$\sigma$~\cite{auger_apj2024_anisotropy}, with the dipolar amplitude increasing with energy as expected from a shrinking ``horizon'' due to propagation effects.
Although, when searching for anisotropies at small and intermediate scales, a 5$\sigma$ detection has not yet been reached, with respective significances of 4.2$\sigma$ for a catalog of starburst galaxy locations~\cite{icrc2025_anisotropy} and 4.0$\sigma$ for the Centaurus A region~\cite{icrc2023_geraldina_anisotropy}.
Current Auger analysis models are attempting to combine mass information with arrival directions to perform mass-informed anisotropy studies, showing with simulations that rigidity-dependent anisotropies are detectable with the Pierre Auger Observatory~\cite{uhecr2024_lorenzo, uhecr2024_emily, icrc2025_geraldina_anisotropy}.
An application to Auger data will come soon, the results of which can be used to further inform source models of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays and their acceleration processes.

\section{Propagation Effects}\label{sec:propagation}
Probing cosmic-ray sources and their source evolution becomes complicated at ultra-high-energies due to cosmic-ray energy losses due to propagation effects.
Above an energy of approximately \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{18}}{\electronvolt}, cosmic ray interactions with background photon fields become the dominant energy loss mechanism over the adiabatic energy losses from the expansion of the Universe.
Above this threshold, proton cosmic rays will begin to interact with the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) via Bethe-Heitler pair production:

\begin{equation}
  p + \gamma_{\text{CMB}} \rightarrow p + e^{+} + e^{-}, \label{eq:cmb_pairproduction}
\end{equation}

\noindent
which can impact the cosmic-ray energy spectrum and its features~\cite{blumenthal_prd1970}.
At roughly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{7$\times$10^{19}}{\electronvolt}, photopion production:

\begin{equation}
  p + \gamma_{\text{CMB}} \rightarrow p + \pi^{0}, \label{eq:cmb_photopion}
\end{equation}

\noindent
becomes the dominant energy loss effect.
This effect, known as the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK) effect, suppresses the flux of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays above this threshold~\cite{greisen_prl1966_gzk, zatsepin_jetp1966_gzk} and influences the observed cutoff in the cosmic-ray energy spectrum occurring near \qty[parse-numbers=false]{5$\times$10^{19}}{\electronvolt}.
Further, the energy loss length of photopion production decreases with increasing energy, until a plateau in the energy loss length of nearly \qty{20}{\mega\parsec} is reached above cosmic-ray energies of \qty[parse-numbers=false]{3$\times$10^{20}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{allard_astropartphys2011_propagation}.
Hence the large scale structure of the Universe is opaque to ultra-high-energy cosmic rays, as referenced in the previous section with the approximate \qty{100}{\mega\parsec} horizon for the sources of the most energetic cosmic rays.

Cosmic ray nuclei (i.e. those heavier than proton) also interact with photon backgrounds via the energy loss processes of adiabatic expansion of the Universe and Bethe-Heitler pair production; however, they interact via a third energy loss process specific to nuclei interactions with photons: photodisintegration.
Photodisintegration processes are those where a background photon is Lorentz boosted to gamma ray energies in the rest frame of the ultra-high-energy cosmic ray nucleus, resulting in the excitation of the nucleus to a nuclear resonance which decays rapidly via the ejection of one or more nucleons or particles, namely

\begin{equation}
  \,_{Z}^{A}X + \gamma_{\text{BGL}} \rightarrow \,_{Z - Z^{\prime}}^{A - A^{\prime}}X^{\prime} + b, \label{eq:cmb_photodisintegration}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where the subscript BGL represents a photon from any background light field.
Both the CMB and infrared background radiation are important photon fields for this interaction~\cite{puget_apj1976_photodisintegration, stecker_apj1999_photodisintegration}.
The energy threshold for these photodisintegration processes is proportional to the mass number $A$ of the cosmic-ray nucleus, therefore this extragalactic propagation process could impose a mass dependent ordering on the composition of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays observed at Earth~\cite{tom_book}.
A more complete theoretical description of photonuclear interactions for understanding cosmic-ray propagation is given in~\cite{rachen_phdthesis}.
A full description of all understood cosmic-ray propagation processes is included in CRPropa~\cite{rafa_crpropa}, a state-of-the-art Monte Carlo code for simulating cosmic-ray extragalactic and Galactic propagation.

\section{Sources, Acceleration Mechanisms, and MM}\label{sec:sources}
Using the combined information from the previous sections, physicists can attempt to answer the most outstanding question in cosmic-ray physics, where do the highest energy cosmic rays come from?
In attempts to describe the data, theories suggesting how the acceleration of particles, both within and outside our Galaxy, to such high energies have been made.
Fermi hypothesized that charged particles passing through moving and magnetized plasma, such as ionized gas clouds or shock fronts, will gain energy through multiple scatterings within the gas cloud or passings across the shock front~\cite{fermi_acceleration}.
The resulting spectrum expected from Fermi acceleration is a constant power law, close in numeric value to the spectral index from cosmic-ray observations.
Models of supernova remnants (SNR) generate these shock fronts and provide adequate time periods and power for cosmic rays within the Milky Way galaxy to be accelerated to near knee-like energies~\cite{jorg_astropartphys2004_kneemodels}.

Yet, deviations in the cosmic-ray energy spectrum from a constant power law hint at changes in the cosmic-ray source populations or acceleration mechanisms and, when combined with knowledge of the mass composition, anisotropy, and propagation effects, provide a full data-drive and theoretical framework of understanding cosmic-ray origins.
Historically, models of the change in spectral index at the knee in the cosmic-ray energy spectrum were driven by the assumption of a magnetic rigidity dependent cutoff for the acceleration and propagation of sources.
The dependence on the energy spectrum of this magnetic rigidity $R_{\text{B}}$, given by

\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{B}} = \frac{pc}{Ze} \approx \frac{E}{Ze}, \label{eq:rigidity}
\end{equation}

\noindent
was first proposed by Peters in 1961, and is since known as a Peters cycle~\cite{peters_1961}.
In~\Cref{eq:rigidity}, $E$ is the energy of the cosmic-ray nucleus and $Z$ its atomic number, which, in the context of a maximum energy cutoff for a source population or propagation process is directly dependent on the cosmic-ray charge ($Z$).
The ``turning off'' of these sources and processes then leads to spectral softenings of the individual nuclear components at separate energies, with lighter cosmic rays softening at lower energies than heavier cosmic rays, and could contribute to the observed knee and second knee spectral features~\cite{kascade_astropartphys2005_knee, kascadegrande_prl2011_secondknee}
In an update to~\cite{kascadegrande_prl2011_secondknee}, an increase of a light component in the cosmic-ray flux was observed near this second knee feature, hinting at a ``turning on'' of extragalactic source populations.
The exact transition of the Galactic to extragalactic origin of cosmic rays is still unknown, yet is occurs somewhere between the knee and ankle.

LHAASO has precisely measured both the proton and helium spectra in the energy region surrounding the cosmic-ray knee, reporting first a spectral hardening before a spectral softening for both nuclei~\cite{lhaaso_scib2025_protonknee, lhaaso_prl2025_heliumknee}.
This challenges the assumption of a single Peters cycle source evolution near the knee and could hint at either: 1. a second Galactic source population, or 2. a Galactic propagation effect.
With the recent LHAASO results, theories suggesting direct spectral signatures of PeVatons, sources accelerating cosmic rays up to PeV energies, could be visible at the cosmic-ray knee~\cite{fang_apjl2026_pevatrons}.
The increased popularity of PeVatron studies in the field and the extreme precision of LHAASO has led to potential associations of, for example, pulsar wind nebula with Galactic PeVatrons~\cite{dikerby_apj2025_pwn}.
More than likely, the knee (and other spectral features) are a superposition of both acceleration and propagation effects; however, associating astrophysical objects as Galactic PeVatrons is one step closer towards determining cosmic-ray origins across the cosmic-ray energy spectrum.
