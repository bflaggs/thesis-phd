\chapter{Neural Network Based Reconstructions of a Muon Observable}\label{chap:nn}
The previous chapter highlights the importance of combining radio $X_{\rm max}$ and muon measurements for the prospect of increasing the event-by-event mass separation between primary cosmic-ray mass groups.
At the Pierre Auger Observatory, an event-level $X_{\rm max}$ reconstruction using the radio technique is well established, with results from AERA already published~\cite{auger_prd2024_aeraxmax,auger_prl2024_aeraxmax}.
The UMD provides direct muon measurements of air showers on a per-event basis, which in principle can be combined with per-event radio $X_\text{max}$ reconstructions to increase the overall mass sensitivity of Auger, both per-event and for the average mass composition.
The AERA and UMD footprints are co-located making simultaneous measurements feasible; however, implementation is difficult due to both geometrical and timeline restrictions for each detector system.
The geometry of UMD scintillator panels is optimal for vertical air-shower reconstructions while the spacing of the AERA radio antennas makes the AERA $X_{\rm max}$ reconstruction more efficient for air showers with $\theta_{\rm zen}\,>\,$\qty{40}{\degree} (see~\Cref{fig:aera_umd_histograms}).
Regarding timeline constraints, AERA, recording air showers since 2011, has fulfilled its primary science goal of proving the usefulness of air-shower reconstructions in the \qtyrange{30}{80}{\mega\hertz} band near Malarg\"{u}e and will soon enter the decomissioning phase.
The UMD did not obtain a stable reconstruction until January 2018, with deployment only finishing in March 2026, severely limiting the data overlap between the two reconstruction methods.
The radio $X_{\rm max}$ reconstruction will eventually be ported to the RD antennas, deployed at each Auger SD, yet the spacing of RD will incur the same reconstruction efficiency constraint as AERA.

Instead a neural network based method to reconstruct a per-event muon observable, to be later combined with previously recorded AERA measurements, is investigated over the following two chapters.
A previous Auger analysis has used deep learning methods to reconstruct the station-level muonic component of the PMT traces bin-by-bin for the SD-1500~\cite{auger_jinst2021_dnnmuons,juanmiguel_phd2020}.
The general concept is driven from the knowledge that muons in air showers arrive at ground faster and, on average, with larger energies than their electromagnetic counterparts.
Muonic particles reaching the Auger water-Cherenkov detectors generate Cherenkov light within the water tank that produces sharp peaks over short time windows within the measured PMT traces.
The characteristics of this ``fast and spiky'' muonic component of the PMT traces are difficult to disentangle from their electromagnetic counterpart, yet can be distinguished using deep learning methods. 
This chapter updates the previous deep learning analysis to predict the station-level muon traces of the SD-750 array, essentially lowering the energy threshold of the prediction by one decade in energy.
Another analysis had updated the SD-1500 muon trace predictions including station ageing effects, yet this is not investigated here for the SD-750 as it requires a large library of highly detailed detector simulations~\cite{orazio_phd2023}.

\section{Network Architecture and Inputs}\label{sec:architecture}
The network architecture was inherited from~\cite{auger_jinst2021_dnnmuons} and is classified as a conditional recurrent neural network (RNN), meaning static features set the initial state and inform the hidden layers of the time-series prediction blocks of the RNN.
The RNN block is formed by three long short-term memory (LSTM) layers that process information sequentially, predicting the outcome at the present time based on knowledge of the previous time evolution of the dataset.
The static features are first progressed through two fully connected, i.e. dense, layers to encode their information and are then combined with the first LSTM block to set the initial conditions and hidden states of the time evolution.
The RNN then passes through two additional LSTM layers, both with their hidden and cell states initialized to zero meaning the only input comes from the previous LSTM layer.
Finally, the output of the third LSTM layer is sliced to only take the first of 32 features to be transposed and fed to a final dense layer.
This transpose and final dense layer removes the strict causality requirement of the temporal data from in the LSTM blocks, allowing a bidirectional flow of predictive information across the time series.
A bidirectional LSTM architecture would induce a similar effect.

The input to the network is a vector of length 203.
Three of the inputs are static features and the remaining 200 constitute a single time series vector spanning 200 time bins.
The goal of the conditional RNN is to predict the muonic component of the Auger water-Cherenkov detector PMT traces at the station level, therefore static variables with a high dependence on the air-shower muon number are chosen.
The static variables are the station distance to the air-shower axis ($r$), the azimuthal angle in the shower plane between the station and the incoming air-shower direction ($\zeta$), and the secant of the air-shower zenith angle ($\sec\theta$).
The end goal is a station-level result, therefore the inputs should also be station level to prevent any unexpected event-level dependencies from leaking into the analysis.
However, the zenith angle as an input feature is unavoidable, as this observable is directly related to the atmospheric attenuation of the air-shower particles.

The time-series input is the first 200 times bins of the PMT traces from a single Auger water-Cherenkov detector, averaged over all active PMTs for that station.
All analysis/training will be performed using the Phase I data/simulations of the Pierre Auger Observatory, recorded with the UB electronics of the Auger SD.
These 200 bins then correspond to the first \qty{5}{\micro\second} of the \qty{19.2}{\micro\second} long PMT traces.
The previous analyses show using 200 time bins provides an optimal mix of speed and accuracy, as 99\% of the station's muonic component is recorded in the first \qty{5}{\micro\second} for air showers with energies less than \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{19}}{\electronvolt}~\cite{auger_jinst2021_dnnmuons, juanmiguel_phd2020}.
The final output of the network is a time series of 200 bins predicting the bin-by-bin muon content of the input time series. 
The architecture, inputs, and predictions are the same as those reported in~\cite{orazio_phd2023}, minus the station age as a static input feature.
\Cref{fig:architecture} shows a schematic of the network.
Note that all dense layers in the architecture have a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function while all LSTM layers use a hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation function.

\section{Training Dataset}\label{sec:data}
As with any machine learning analysis, understanding and preprocessing the training dataset is a necessary step to ensure proper predictions output by the network and can inform the choice of network hyperparameters.
The network architecture described in the previous section is trained using CORSIKA simulations passed through \Offline, the analysis software of the Pierre Auger Observatory~\cite{offline_nima2007, auger_nima2011_radiooffline}.
CORSIKA simulation are used to generate the air-shower signals, both in the atmosphere and at ground, seen by the various detectors of Auger.
\Offline then simulates the full detector response of the signal recorded by each detector system selected for analysis by the configuration of \Offline.
After simulating the full detector responses, the data are combined into a single Auger Data Summary Table (ADST) file.
ADSTs from \Offline mimic the data storage of the fully processed data of the Auger observatory, therefore allowing high-level analyses on this output.

%ADSTs have the same format used to store the true data of the observatory, therefore allowing high-level analyses on this output.

\subsection{Summary of the Dataset}\label{ssec:data_summary}
The software version used for this analysis is the \Offline icrc2025-test7 version configured for a UMD and SD-750 production of Auger Phase I data.
The simulations and \Offline productions available for this analysis were generated by the Auger collaboration's Monte Carlo task group and are freely available for use for any member of the collaboration\footnote[1]{The description and \Offline configuration of this production are available for members of the Pierre Auger Observatory at the link: \href{https://www.auger.unam.mx/AugerWiki/NapoliLibrary\#head-f39e6bd21998ef5a242dd9f7b33fbc10abc1642f}{https://www.auger.unam.mx/AugerWiki/NapoliLibrary}.}.
The production is based on CORSIKA v7.8010 air showers simulated with hadronic models EPOS LHC-R, Sibyll 2.3e, and QGSJETIII-01, where all low-energy hadronic interactions were simulated with FLUKA.
For each hadronic model, simulations are available for proton, helium, oxygen, and iron.
For each primary, 5000 showers were simulated for each of two energy bins, \qtyrange[parse-numbers=false]{10^{17.5}}{10^{18}}{\electronvolt} and \qtyrange[parse-numbers=false]{10^{18}}{10^{18.5}}{\electronvolt}, where zenith angles range between \qtyrange{0}{65}{\degree} within each bin.
The energy spectra and zenith angles within each bin are distributed with $dN/dE \propto E^{-1}$ and $dN/d\theta \propto \sin\theta\cos\theta$ respectively, resulting in a flat distribution in $\lg(E / \rm eV)$ for the energies and a flat distribution in $\sin^{2}(\theta)$ for the zenith angles, similar to the simulation library used in~\Cref{chap:simulations}.
Within each energy bin, the 5000 simulated showers are split evenly between different atmospheric models, resulting in 1250 showers for each of the January, March, August, and September atmospheres in Malarg\"{u}e, Argentina.

Atmospheric changes in temperature and pressure directly impact the attenuation of particles and muon production within the atmosphere, inducing a seasonal effect on the absolute number of muons in air showers. CITESTEF. 
Furthermore, changes in temperature also affect the VEM calibration of water-Cherenkov detectors (see~\Cref{app:layered_wcd}) and hence have a direct impact on the muonic signal measured by ground-based detectors.
In this analysis, the impact on the network predictions from differences in the atmospheric models is not studied, yet will be crucial to study in the future to fully capture minute details in the muon signal measured by the SD.
Further updates to the network could include expanding the architecture to train on and provide predictions for Auger Phase II data productions. 
The upgraded SD electronics used for Phase II data increased the data sampling rate (see~\Cref{sec:augerprime}), therefore to encapsulate the same physics in the network using Phase II data/simulations would require an increase in the length of the input time series.
Currently, there is not an available simulation production of both the UMD and SD-750 for Auger Phase II data using the most current hadronic interaction models.

\Offline places the simulated core of each air shower in the library at random within the footprint of the SD-750 array and then runs the SD-750 and UMD reconstructions, both following the icrc2025-test7 standard module sequences for the respective detector system.
The event information for each reconstruction is stored in an ADST, along with the individual station-level information, as the network architecture trains and predicts at the station level.
For each station, the first 200 time bins (\qty{5}{\micro\second}) of the total signal measured by the PMTs is averaged over all active PMTs and saved in the ADST.
Nominally, the number of active PMTs is three.
These averaged traces will be used as the time-series input of the neural network.

\subsection{Data Selection}\label{ssec:selection}
An adage in machine learning, and one of my favorite sayings about these analyses: ``if you put crap in, you get crap out.''
Following this dogma, a high-quality subset of the station-level simulated traces must be selected to train the RNN.
This subset should mimic measured data traces of high quality, as eventually the network will be applied to real measured traces from SD-750 reconstructed air showers.
For training and validating the network, only the \Offline production for EPOS LHC-R simulations will be used.
Therefore this section, and the sections following it, only contain results for simulations using this hadronic model unless otherwise stated. 

The selection cuts applied to the dataset are the same as in~\cite{orazio_phd2023} and will be described briefly here.
Some of the selection cuts are applied at the event level to ensure high-quality data for the full event, while others are applied at the station (i.e. trace) level to ensure the signal in the station is of acceptable quality.
The event-level selections require a converged LDF fit and a 6T5 trigger for the event.
An additional event-level selection requires the reconstructed zenith angle to be $<$\qty{58}{\degree}.
This cut was not used in~\cite{orazio_phd2023}, yet must be applied here due to the poor quality of SD-750 reconstructions for inclined air showers.
\Cref{fig:zenith_outliers} highlights this for the SD-750, showing a histogram of the reconstructed zenith angle for all simulations with a difference in the reconstructed and Monte Carlo truth (MC) energies greater than half a decade in energy.
Furthermore, the end goal is to predict the muonic signal for air showers between \qtyrange{0}{55}{\degree}.
Therefore training on a larger zenith range will eliminate possible edge effects in the network's predictive power.
The \qty{55}{degree} limit is set from the ``bias-free'' AERA $X_{\rm max}$ data selection, which applies a zenith angle selection of $<$\qty{55}{\degree} on both the SD and AERA directional reconstructions.

\subsection{Studying the Training and Validation Sets Pre-Training}\label{ssec:pretraining}
%Training and testing results, testing for biases
The final 1,280,885 traces after selection cuts from~\Cref{tab:cuts} were split randomly into training and testing subsets with a respective 80\%-20\% split.
The larger training set was then further split randomly, again using an 80\%-20\% split, into the final training subset and a validation subset.
The validation subset was used to analyze the results of the trained RNN predictions on simulations not yet seen by the network, allowing the network to go through multiple iterations of training and updating the hyperparameters typical of training neural networks (see~\Cref{sec:training} for details).
Both the train-test and train-validation splits were performed event wise to ensure stations from the same event occupy the same data subset.
This serves two purposes.
On the one hand, the network performance can be studied as a function of event-level observables, such as energy and zenith angle, with confidence that any biases visible in the performance plots are from the network predictions rather than biases arising from the data splits used.
On the other hand, the final analysis plans to take the station-level network predictions of the muonic signal, combine the predictions for single events, and fit a muon LDF to the station predictions for each event, therefore extracting an event-level muon observable from a station-level prediction.
This event-level splitting of the datasets preserves the information necessary to make this plan feasible.
After splitting the subsets event wise, the training set contains 822,099 traces, the validation set contains 203,297 traces, and the testing set contains 255,489 traces.

Yet, the SD-750 reconstructed energy underestimates the MC energy by $\sim$20\% across the full energy range of the training dataset.
The reconstruction-MC mismatch does not represent an unknown bias from the selection cuts; however, is an expected bias due to the muon deficit~\cite{schmidt_privcomm_2026}.
MC simulations generate lower muon content than what is measured in data for the same air-shower energy, thereby generating less signal at ground measurable by the SD array.
Applying the SD energy reconstruction to simulations then results in an energy estimate systematically lower than the true MC energy because the SD energy reconstruction uses a measurement-derived calibration, as described in~\Cref{sec:reconstruction}.
This underestimation compared to MC is also seen in the SD-1500 reconstructed energies~\cite{auger_gap2025_muondeficit}.
The energy reconstruction itself will not introduce any biases in the network predictions because the energy is not an input to the network, although the muon deficit will.
Bias from the muon deficit can not be corrected for because of the use of simulations in the network training, and instead induces a systematic uncertainty on the result.

\section{Network Training}\label{sec:training}
% Preprocessing here or in a subsection of the previous section names Final Preprocessing
The final data preprocessing step before training the network is to scale the network inputs to similar levels.
This allows the network to assign weighting factors to features based on percentage differences rather than absolute differences, essentially assigning an equal weighting factor to the same percentage difference regardless of the studied input feature.
Other purposes serve to increase the speed at which the network converges and to provide a numerical range favorable for the chosen activation function of the network.
All inputs to the network are min-max scaled $\in$[0,1] with the exception of $r$.
$r$ is instead divided by \qty{1500}{\meter} to approximately scale the $r$ values within this same range.

The origin for $\zeta$ occurs in the azimuthal direction of the incoming air shower, constraining the $\zeta$ phase space to [$-\pi$,$\pi$].
The $\zeta$ values are therefore shifting by $\pi$ to occupy a fully positive phase space before scaling.
More importantly, the averaged PMT traces used as the time series input to the network can contain negative signals due to baseline fluctuations or incorrectly subtracted baselines from errors in the generalized subtraction procedure.
The trace bins with negative signal are explicitly set to zero pre-training to mitigate this issue.
Each trace is scaled individually by its maximum value, fixing the trace input also within the range of [0,1].
The trace maximum values are saved to rescale the network outputs.

An optimization process of selecting network hyperparameters and re-training the network was implemented until a final selection of hyperparameters resulted in a final network training.
The network implementation was built using \texttt{PyTorch} version 1.13.1~\cite{pytorch_neurips2019} and was trained on an NVIDIA A100 GPU.
Training for the network takes between \qtyrange{1}{2}{\hour} depending on the chosen hyperparameters, with the final training taking \qty{1.06}{\hour} in total.
The loss function used in training is a root mean square error (MSE) function of the form

\begin{equation}
    L = \sqrt{\frac{1}{200}\sum_{t=1}^{200}\left(\hat{S}_{t}^{\mu} - S_{t}^{\mu}\right)^{2}} \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\hat{S}_{t}^{\mu}$ is the neural network predicted muonic signal of the trace in time bin $t$, and $S_{t}^{\mu}$ is the true muonic signal of the trace in time bin $t$.
The training dataset is split into batches where the loss is calculated per batch of data and then summed over the full training dataset.
A batch size of 512 was used in the final network training. 
Also tested to train the network was a batch size of 1024, yet this was not used in the final training as it provided no improvement on network performance.

During the training, an adaptive movement estimation (Adam) optimizer was used.
The optimizer actively adjusts the individual learning rates of network input features based on the moving average of the loss function gradients and their squares.
A gradient clipping with a normalization of 1.0 was added to the optimizer to stabilize the network training by preventing extreme loss function gradients.
The addition of gradient clipping added little to no improvement on network training, yet was maintained to prevent unstable or divergent loss functions in future network implementations.
A baseline learning rate of 0.001 was used and set with a learning rate scheduler which decreases this learning rate by 0.5 if there is no loss improvement after 10 epochs of training.

\Cref{fig:loss_curves} shows the evolution of the training and validation loss functions with training epoch.
The sharp visible drops in loss are epochs where the learning rate scheduler reduced the learning rate.
Smoothness of the training loss curve shows a stable training of the network is achieved, while some fluctuation on the validation loss is expected from the network's blindness to the validation data during training.
The plateau of the validation loss highlights the network has learned all general information useful for the network prediction that is shared between the training data and a before unseen dataset.
Final training is reached after 150 epochs of training and is stopped here to prevent overfitting of specific features within the training dataset.

\section{Network Performance}\label{sec:performance}
The general network performance on the validation set is studied after final training of the network.
This performance can also be studied as a function of epoch during the training of the network.
\Cref{fig:metric_training} shows the average difference between the total predicted muon signal ($\hat{S}^{\mu}$) and true muon signal ($S^{\mu}$) in the PMTs, and the spread in this difference, as a function of training epoch for the validation data.
As the final epoch is approached the average difference approaches roughly \qty{0}{VEM}, indicating little bias in the network's predicted muon signals when averaged over all traces; however, the standard deviation of the difference distribution approaches roughly \qty{5}{VEM} indicating a large spread in differences over the full range of values.
This standard deviation is calculated for all primaries, zenith angles, distances to the air-shower axis, etc. in the validation set and therefore a large spread is expected.

The network performance is evaluated using the relative difference metric, defined as

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\hat{S}^{\mu} - S^{\mu}}{S^{\mu}}. \label{eq:metric}
\end{equation}

\noindent
$\hat{S}^{\mu}$ is the total, bin-by-bin summed, muon signal in the trace predicted by the neural network and $S^{\mu}$ is the MC truth of this value.
The relative difference is histogrammed individually for each primary (p, He, O, Fe) of the EPOS LHC-R validation set, shown in~\Cref{fig:relative_difference}.
The bias ($\mu$) and resolution ($\sigma$) are included for each histogram, along with a dashed line at a relative difference of 0.0 to represent no bias.
Proton has the largest positive bias which decreases with the mass ordering to iron with the largest negative bias.
Similarly, the resolution decreases as primary mass increases, with proton having the largest resolution.
Proton showers have larger shower-to-shower fluctuations compared to heavier primaries therefore these results are not unexpected.
Visible are asymmetries in the relative difference histograms, arising from dependencies on other event and station variables.
Investigating the evolution of this metric with each variable is a necessary step to understand the limitation of the network's accuracy in various phase spaces.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[trim={0cm 0cm 0cm 0cm},clip,width=1.0\textwidth]{chapter5/metric_histogram_relative_delta_muon_signal_2x2primaries_fulldataset.pdf}
  \caption[Histograms of the muon signal relative difference.]{Histograms of the relative difference metric presented in~\Cref{eq:metric}. Each histogram is for all stations for a single primary particle (i.e. over all energies, zenith angles, etc.). $\mu$ is the mean relative difference for that primary and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. These are respectively known as the bias and resolution of the network within the given phase space they are calculated over. A dashed vertical line at a relative difference of 0.0 represents no bias, meaning the network exactly predicts the total muonic signal in the station as compared to simulations.}
  \label{fig:relative_difference}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:mc_energy} shows the bias and resolution plots using the relative difference metric,~\Cref{eq:metric}, for all four primaries as a function of the Monte Carlo air-shower energy.
A bias of zero represents an RNN that exactly predicts the true muon signal per station, while a resolution of zero represents no spread on the RNN predictions.
A mathematical corollary to a metric for an RNN with both a bias and resolution of zero would be a dirac delta function.
The bias shows a spread in the individual mass groups centered around zero bias.
Proton simulations have the largest bias and over-predict the muon signals on average, while iron simulations have the most negative bias and under-predict the muon signals on average.
This behavior is expected, as iron initiated air showers have on average more muons than the other studied primaries, with proton initiated showers having the least amount of muons on average.
As the network was trained on nearly average mass fractions, the expectation would be for the network bias averaged over all primaries to sit between the two extremes of proton and iron, as seen in the figure.
The resolution plot shows proton has the largest resolution, with decreasing resolution as mass increases to iron, which has the smallest resolution.
This behavior is foreseen due to protons having larger shower-to-shower fluctuations compared to iron air showers.
Important to note is the network resolution decreases with increasing air-shower energy.
The plots in~\Cref{fig:mc_energy} therefore show stable network predictive power as a function of air-shower energy, with less stability for lower energy air showers.

\Cref{fig:sd_zenith} shows the bias and resolution per primary in bins of the secant of the SD-750 reconstructed zenith angle ($\sec\theta_{\rm SD}$),~\Cref{fig:sd_r} shows these plots for the reconstructed distance of the station to the shower core ($r_{\rm SD}$), and~\Cref{fig:sd_zeta} shows these same plots for the reconstructed azimuthal angle between the station and the air-shower axis in the shower plane ($\zeta_{\rm SD}$).
The same y-axis limits are chosen between all bias plots, and similarly for all resolution plots, allowing for by eye comparisons of the bias and resolutions between observables.
Similar primary distributions as in~\Cref{fig:mc_energy} (over-predictions for proton and under-predictions for iron, with everything else in between) are seen.
The most notable bias plot is that of $r_{\rm SD}$, which shows all primaries approaching a bias of zero then deviating from their general trendlines in the bins farthest from the shower axis.
This is at least expected in the final distance bin due to lesser statistics compared to other bins.
The resolution plot of $r_{\rm SD}$ is also notable, as the RNN resolution for all primaries other than proton increases with increasing distance from the air-shower axis.
Further dependencies on the other observables exists within each bias and resolution plot and need to be accounted for to more completely describe the predictive accuracy and precision of the RNN (see~\Cref{ssec:parameterization} for a full description.)
