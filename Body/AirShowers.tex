\chapter{Cosmic-Ray Air Showers}\label{chap:airshowers}
Cosmic rays above roughly \qty[parse-numbers=false]{10^{14}}{\electronvolt} entering Earth's atmosphere will interact with atmospheric nuclei to initiate extensive air showers, cascades of secondary particles and electromagnetic radiation.
The primary cosmic ray is no longer directly detectable after initiating this cascade; however, features of the primary cosmic ray, such as its energy, mass, and arrival direction, are imparted on the air shower's constituent particles and radiation.
The air-shower constituents continue to interact in the atmosphere, which acts as a calorimetric medium during the shower development, until reaching Earth's surface where they are detected using a variety of techniques.
This chapter will introduce the physics of air showers, including air-shower modeling and the relation of air-shower components to the primary cosmic ray, while~\Cref{chap:auger} will introduce the air-shower detection techniques implemented by the Pierre Auger Observatory.

\section{Air-Shower Development}\label{sec:development}
Air showers develop in the atmosphere along the air-shower axis, the straight line that follows the trajectory of the primary cosmic ray prior to its first atmospheric interaction.
The first interactions are of the highest energy and generate high-energy particles beamed in the forward direction parallel to the shower axis.
Subsequent interactions generate a larger number of particles than the previous generation of interactions, resulting in more and more particles of lower energy with each generation.
Particles resulting from these interactions can receive a significant transverse momentum in relation to the shower axis, depending upon the nature of the generating collision, which propagates throughout the shower development and contributes to a lateral spread of the air shower.
The absolute particle number of the air shower continues to grow as the shower develops longitudinally until the particle generation processes are no longer energetically favorable.
After this threshold, the particle number in the air shower decreases due to atmospheric attenuation until the shower front reaches Earth's surface.
Knowledge of atmospheric physics and particle physics is necessary to understand the development of an air shower, while simplified models of air showers can provide zeroth-order approximations relating the primary cosmic ray and the air shower it produces.

\subsection{The Atmosphere}\label{ssec:atmosphere}
The development of an air shower depends on the amount of atmospheric material traversed as the shower develops, referred to as the atmospheric slant depth or simply slant depth, given by

\begin{equation}
    X = \int_{\ell}^{\infty} \rho\left( \ell^{\prime} \right) d\,\ell^{\prime} \label{eq:slant}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\ell$ is the distance along the air-shower axis and $\rho\left(\ell\right)$ is the density profile of the atmosphere along the shower axis.
The relation between the vertical altitude $h$ above sea level and $\ell$ is purely geometric and determined by the law of cosines:

\begin{equation}
    h = \sqrt{R_{\oplus}^{2} + \ell^{2} + 2\,R_{\oplus}\,\ell\,\cos\theta} - R_{\oplus} \approx \ell\,\cos\theta + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\ell^{2}}{R_{\oplus}}\sin^{2}\theta \label{eq:law_of_cosines}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $R_{\oplus}$ is the radius of the Earth and $\theta$ is the zenith angle of the air shower, the angle between the lines of local zenith directly beneath the point of first interaction in the atmosphere and the air-shower axis.
The approximation in~\Cref{eq:law_of_cosines} is a first-order Taylor expansion (i.e.~truncated after two terms) taken in the limit $\ell << R_{\oplus}$.

For a completely vertical air shower, with a zenith angle $\theta$\,=\,\qty{0}{\degree}, the traversed distance along the shower axis becomes the vertical altitude $h$ as measured above sea level and the slant depth becomes the vertical atmospheric depth, or simply atmospheric depth,

\begin{equation}
    X_{v} = \int_{h}^{\infty} \rho\left( h^{\prime} \right) d\,h^{\prime}, \label{eq:vertical_depth}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where both the atmospheric and slant depths are reported in units of $\text{g}/\text{cm}^{2}$.
For reference, assuming an isothermal atmosphere and an average altitude of \qty{1400}{\meter} for the Pierre Auger Observatory in Malarg\"{u}e, Argentina yields an atmospheric depth of approximately \qty{875}{\gram\per\centi\meter\squared}.
In reality, the atmosphere exhibits seasonal variations and its temperature varies with altitude, both described extensively within atmospheric profile models used in air-shower simulations for theoretical studies and data validation.

For air showers with $\theta<$\,\qty{65}{\degree} the zeroth-order approximation from~\Cref{eq:law_of_cosines} can be used to determine the slant depth, giving

\begin{equation}
    X = \frac{X_{v}}{\cos\theta} = X_{v}\,\sec\theta. \label{eq:slant_depth}
\end{equation}

\noindent
This approximation assumes the atmosphere and Earth are flat, which no longer holds for $\theta>$\,\qty{65}{\degree} and instead requires the full treatment from~\Cref{eq:law_of_cosines}.
However, the approximation in~\Cref{eq:slant_depth} highlights the direct effect of zenith angle on atmospheric material traversed, which in turn impacts the attenuation of particles during the development of an air shower.

\subsection{The Cascade Equations}\label{ssec:cascade_equations}
The longitudinal development of an air shower is modeled using a set of differential equations known as the cascade equations.
The cascade equations describe the differential flux of the the number of particles of type $i$, as a function of the particle energies $E_{i}$ at a slant depth $X$ as

\begin{align}
    \frac{\text{d}N_{i}\left(E_{i}, X\right)}{\text{d}X} = & -\frac{N_{i}\left(E_{i}, X\right)}{\lambda_{i}} - \frac{N_{i}\left(E_{i}, X\right)}{d_{i}} \nonumber\\
    & + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \int_{E}^{\infty} \frac{F_{ji}\left(E_{i}, E_{j}\right)}{E_{i}} \, \frac{N_{j}\left(E_{j}, X\right)}{\lambda_{j}} \, \text{d}E_{j} \label{eq:cascade}
\end{align}

\noindent
with $\lambda_{i}$ and $d_{i}$ the respective interaction and decay lengths of particle type $i$.
The sum over $j$ represents a sum over all possible parent particles that interact to generate particles of type $i$ and contribute to the differential flux.
Related, the function $F_{ji}\left(E_{i}, E_{j}\right)$ is a dimensionless measure of the particle production from a particle of type $j$ and energy $E_{j}$ colliding with a molecule in the atmosphere to produce a particle of type $i$ and energy $E_{i}$.
This function is explicitly defined as

\begin{equation}
    F_{ji}\left(E_{i}, E_{j}\right) = E_{i} \frac{1}{\sigma_{j\,\text{air}}^{\text{had}}} \frac{\text{d}\sigma_{j\,\text{air}\rightarrow i}}{\text{d}E_{i}} \label{eq:particle_yield}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\sigma_{j\,\text{air}}^{\text{had}}$ is the total hadronic cross section for interactions between particle type $j$ and atmospheric molecules and $\sigma_{j\,\text{air}\rightarrow i}$ is the inclusive cross section for interactions of particle type $j$ and atmospheric molecules that produce a particle of type $i$.
These interactions are limited to $E_{i}<E_{j}$ due to conservation of energy.

The interaction length is dependent upon the total hadronic cross section:

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{i} = \frac{A\,m_{p}}{\sigma_{j\,\text{air}}^{\text{had}}} \label{eq:interaction_length}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $A$ is the mean mass of atmospheric nuclei and $m_{p}$ is the proton mass.
Alternatively, the decay length is proportional to the atmospheric density at altitude $h$:

\begin{equation}
    d_{i} = \rho(h)\,\gamma\,c\,\tau_{i} \label{eq:decay_length}
\end{equation}

\noindent
with $\tau_{i}$ the lifetime at rest of particle type $i$ and $\gamma$ the Lorentz factor of the particle.
Note that~\Cref{eq:cascade} does not account for energy losses in the atmosphere due to ionization, which occur naturally as a high-energy charged particle deposits energy by ionizing the medium it traverses.
Ionization energy losses are unimportant for hadrons or high-energy electrons in an air shower; however, they become important for energies at and below the critical energy, the energy where interaction losses from radiative or hadronic processes equal energy losses due to ionization.

An in depth description of the cascade equations, both generally and for specific particle types, is provided in~\cite{tom_book}.
Further, the Matrix Cascade Equations (MCEq) code\footnote[1]{\href{https://github.com/mceq-project/MCEq}{https://github.com/mceq-project/MCEq}} provides a way to numerically solve the cascade equations with computationally efficient methods.

\subsection{The Heitler-Matthews Model}\label{ssec:heitler_matthews}
Cosmic-ray air showers can be described by two separate shower components, an electromagnetic and a hadronic component\footnote[2]{Some literature describe air showers as having three components: electromagnetic, hadronic, and muonic. Here the hadronic and muonic components are considered one, as the muonic component is generated via the hadronic cascade (see~\Cref{sssec:hadronic}).}.
A simplified toy model of air showers, known as the Heitler-Matthews model~\cite{matthews_astropartphys2005_heitlermatthews}, treats the cascades of the electromagnetic and hadronic shower components separately, where the hadronic cascade prolongs the electromagnetic cascade.
This toy model serves to statistically relate attributes of the primary cosmic ray to separate measurable parameters of an air shower, called air-shower observables; however, the exact relationships are complicated and not known precisely for individual air showers.

\subsubsection{Electromagnetic Cascade}\label{sssec:electromagnetic}
The original toy model of electromagnetic cascades in the atmosphere was developed by Heitler~\cite{heitler_book} as a simple splitting model where single electromagnetic particles ($e^{\pm}$, $\gamma$)\footnote[3]{Throughout this dissertation electromagnetic particles include gamma-ray photons ($\gamma$) unless otherwise stated.} split into two electromagnetic particles at every interaction, each with half of the energy of the original particle.
Only a single electromagnetic particle of high energy, $\mathcal{O}(100)$\,MeV and above, initiates the cascade.
Within the cascade, gamma-ray photons in the vicinity of atmospheric nuclei will interact to pair produce an electron-positron pair:

\begin{equation}
    (Z +) \gamma \rightarrow e^{-} + e^{+} \label{eq:pair_production}
